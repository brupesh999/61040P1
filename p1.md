**Application Name:** Gamexplorer

## 1. Problem Framing and Stakeholders

### Domain
The domain of this project is video game discovery and evlauating them as prospective media in an interactive entertainment. Unlike linear media like movies or literature which rqeuire a few hours of passive consumption with no skill barrier, video games require substantial commitments of time (ranging from 30 to over 100 hours per game), a more significant financial investment ($70 per release), and different levels of physical/cognitive skill. This makes the process of choosing and reviewing video games a much more daunting task compared to linear media, and treating the review systems the same leads to innacurate descriptions that can be misleading to the average user.

Because video games are interactive, player satisfaction is multidimensional. Gamers have very different preferences and prioritize different axes of the experience: some value deep tactical combat mechanics with high skill ceilings, while others seek rich narratives or a relaxing audiovisual atmosphere. This means its much harder to rate a game on a single scale and gain any use from this. A game will always comprise of distinct design tradeoffs and have a much larger variety across almost aspects compared to linear media, but we still use the same reviewing concept as movies when reviewing video games today.

### Stakeholders
* **Mechanics-Focused Gamers:** Players who prioritize intricate gameplay systems, combat depth, and physical responsiveness, and are willing to accept lower production value (e.g., dated graphics or lack of voice acting). An example of this might be something like the original Devil May Cry.
* **Story-Driven & Casual Gamers:** Players who care about immersive stories, beautiful graphics, and accessible learning curves, and might be frustrated by an extremely complex battle system. An example of this might be something like Final Fantasy XVI.
* **Indie Game Developers:** On the creators' side, this could help developers who have ideas for very specific and unique gameplay mechanics and don't have a high budget, but their titles might be rated with a low score overall on normal reviewing platforms due to low visual polish.
* **Prospective Buyers:** The general audience for this service would be consumers trying to evaluate whether a game’s specific strengths match their personal priorities before committing time and money, as it is a nontirvial investment.

### Bad Situations

#### Bad Situation 1: The "Flawed Masterpiece"
* **Scenario:** You are a gamer who loves skill-demanding melee combat systems and does not care about rich world building or voice acting. An independent studio releases a game featuring an innovative frame-accurate parry and combat system, but has a surface-level story and no voice acting due to budget constraints. Mainstream aggregate review sites give the game an overall rating of 3 out of 5 stars because reviewers average down the score to reflect its modest visual presentation.
* **Consequence:** You see the 3/5 star average on standard recommendation feeds and skip purchasing the game, because all the top reviews are complaining about the lack of story without appreciating the other aspects of the game. The indie developer suffers poor sales despite having an extremely well-designed combat system, and you miss out on a title that would have been their favorite game of the year.

#### Bad Situation 2: Reviewer Misinterpretation
* **Scenario:** Your friend finishes a 60 hour RPG and wants to leave a review praising its skill tree customization while warning prospective buyers about the fact that the skippable side quests are uninteresting and make the progression of the game seem boring if you force yourself to do them. On existing review platforms, your friend is forced either to assign a single rating like 7/10, or fill out a rigid form containing sections that are irrelevant to their review (*Graphics*, *Soundtrack*, etc.). Your friend either ends up leaving a review that has no way of fully capturing their thoughts or ends up writing a long, essay-like review that new buyers don't feel like reading compared to observing easily-digestible aggregate scores.
* **Consequence:** Existing platforms loses valuable multi dimensional insight on games. Prospective buyers are left with either innacurate reviews or extremely long review sections that require a significant time input just to fully parse and understand whether it would be appealing.

### Corroboration
Discussions across dedicated gaming communities highlight the failure of single-number ratings. On platforms like Reddit (`r/backloggd` and `r/Games`), countless users have made posts about how they chose to play a game despite bad reviews, only to be confused when they found out the game was perfect to them. These "flawed masterpieces" are hidden beneath low aggregate star ratings. These communities express overall frustration over having to mine hundreds of text comments to discover whether a game's low score stems from genuine mechanical flaws or merely secondary budget constraints. As a gamer myself, my friends and I have been hesitant to play games because of bad reviews online but found those games to be life-changing. On the opposite side, we've also played games that have huge cult followings and overwhelmingly positive reviews that we had to put down after an hour or so of gameplay because it wasn't our style.

### Workarounds and Comparables
* **Backloggd:** They market themselves as Letterboxd for games, and use a similar reviewing system as movies like on Letterboxd. It relies on a single overall 1 to 5 star rating scale. Users are forced to manually read through pages of prose reviews to find raters with similar playstyles and priorities.
* **Steam Tags:** Allows users to apply categorical labels (e.g., "RPG", "Psychological Horror"). Tags indicate the *presence* of a feature, but convey zero information regarding its *quality of execution* or *depth*. These tags often don't provide much information by simpling stating a game as an "RPG" or "Roguelike."
* **Metacritic / IGN:** Displays single review scores paired with static review text. Critics weigh dimensions arbitrarily, making aggregate scores non-comparable across different user preference profiles or across different types of games.

### Solution Sketch
**Gamexplorer** is an aspect-based game evaluation and discovery platform. Instead of forcing reviewers into a single scalar score or a rigid set of mandatory categories, Gamexplorer allows users to submit quantitative ratings (1–10) and targeted for **optional, dynamic aspect dimensions** (e.g., *Combat Depth*, *Pacing*, *Visual Atmosphere*, *Difficulty*). Raters only need to evaluate the dimensions they care about. Prospective buyers can then discover games by setting custom multi criteria filters on the specific gameplay dimensions that matter to them.

## 2. Application Pitch

* **Name:** Gamexplorer
* **Motivation:** Single scalar star ratings collapse the multidimensional tradeoffs of video games, which means some stellar aspects of games get hidden behind average overall scores. Because of this, buyers are overwhelmed with long walls of texts as reviews to parse before they can make a decision on whether to invest their time and money into game.
* **Key Features:**
  1. **Sparse Aspect Rating:** Reviewers can evaluate only the gameplay dimensions they care about (e.g., scoring *Combat Depth* 9/10 and *Pacing* 5/10 while skipping *Graphics* entirely). This eliminates reviewer friction and prevents score context collapse.
  2. **Multi-Criteria Dimensional Search:** Prospective buyers can filter the catalog using specific threshold criteria across dimensions (e.g., *Combat Depth* > 8 AND *Learning Curve* > 5), instantly uncovering hidden gems that align with their personal playstyle. Think almost like shopping for clothes, where you can filter by material or length, but for games.
  3. **Aspect Micro-Notes:** Reviewers can attach 1–2 sentence explanations *directly* to individual dimension scores, so prospective buyers can understand their reasoning or context without having to read a full, lengthy review.

## 3. Conceptual Design

### Overview Note on Concept Roles & Type Instantiations

Gamexplorer is built from five independent, modular concepts:

1. `UserAuthenticating`: Handles user identity creation and credential validation.
2. `GameCatalog`: Maintains the canonical catalog of video game entries.
3. `AspectManaging [Game]`: Manages evaluation dimensions associated with specific games. The generic type parameter `Game` is instantiated with `GameCatalog.Game`.
4. `AspectRating [User, Game, Aspect]`: Stores user submitted quantitative scores and optional 1-2 sentence reviews for each specific aspects. `User` is bound to `UserAuthenticating.User`, `Game` to `GameCatalog.Game`, and `Aspect` to `AspectManaging.Aspect`.
5. `AspectFiltering [Game, Aspect]`: Manages user-defined evaluation criteria and matches games whose aggregate aspect ratings meet or exceed specified minimum thresholds.

---

### Concept Specifications

**concept** UserAuthenticating

**purpose** identify users; prevent unauthorized access to user accounts

**principle** after registering a username and password, a user can authenticate with those exact credentials to obtain their user identity

**state**

    a set of Users with
        a unique username String
        a password String

**actions**

register (username: String, password: String) : (user: User)

    where username and password are non-empty and no entry in Users has username equal to username

    then
        create a new user User
        add user to Users with username and password
        return user


    where an entry in Users already has username equal to username

    then
        refuse USERNAME_TAKEN "The requested username is already registered."


authenticate (username: String, password: String) : (user: User)

    where there exists a user in Users with username equal to username and password equal to password

    then
        return user


    where no user in Users matches username and password

    then
        refuse INVALID_CREDENTIALS "Invalid username or password."


**queries**

_getUser (username: String) : (user: User)

    returns the User matching username if present.


---

**concept** GameCatalog

**purpose** maintain a canonical repository of video game entries and basic metadata

**principle** when a game is added with a title, developer, and release year, it receives a unique identifier that can be used to reference the game across the system

**state**

    a set of Games with
        a unique title String
        a developer String
        a releaseYear Number

**actions**

addGame (title: String, developer: String, releaseYear: Number) : (game: Game)

    where title is non-empty and no entry in Games has title equal to title

    then
        create a new game Game
        add game to Games with title, developer, and releaseYear
        return game


    where an entry in Games already has title equal to title

    then
        refuse GAME_EXISTS "A game with this title already exists."


**queries**

_getGame (title: String) : (game: Game)

    returns the Game matching title if present.


_searchGames (query: String) : (games: set of Game)

    returns all games whose title contains the query string.


---

**concept** AspectManaging [Game]

**purpose** define and maintain named evaluation dimensions (aspects) for games

**principle** after an aspect (such as "Combat Depth" or "Pacing") is created for a game, it can be referenced by reviewers to rate that specific dimension of the game

**state**

    a set of Aspects with
        a game Game
        a name String
        a description String
        unique game and name

**actions**

createAspect (game: Game, name: String, description: String) : (aspect: Aspect)

    where name is non-empty and no entry in Aspects has game equal to game and name equal to name

    then
        create a new aspect Aspect
        add aspect to Aspects with game, name, and description
        return aspect


    where an entry in Aspects already exists for game with name equal to name

    then
        refuse ASPECT_EXISTS "This aspect already exists for the specified game."


**queries**

_getAspects (game: Game) : (aspects: set of Aspect)

    returns all defined aspects for the given game.


---

**concept** AspectRating [User, Game, Aspect]

**purpose** allow users to submit quantitative scores and qualitative 1-2 sentence micro notes for specific aspects of a game without requiring ratings on all aspects

**principle** if a user submits a rating (1–10) and an optional micro note for a specific aspect of a game, that rating is stored and can be updated or removed later

**state**

    a set of Ratings with
        a user User
        a game Game
        an aspect Aspect
        a score Number
        a microNote String
        unique user, game, and aspect

**actions**

rateAspect (user: User, game: Game, aspect: Aspect, score: Number, microNote: String) : ()

    where score >= 1 and score <= 10

    then
        if an entry in Ratings exists for user, game, and aspect
            set score of entry to score
            set microNote of entry to microNote
        otherwise
            add a new entry to Ratings with user, game, aspect, score, and microNote


    where score < 1 or score > 10

    then
        refuse INVALID_SCORE "Score must be an integer between 1 and 10."


removeRating (user: User, game: Game, aspect: Aspect) : ()

    where there exists an entry in Ratings matching user, game, and aspect

    then
        remove matching entry from Ratings


    where no entry in Ratings matches user, game, and aspect

    then
        refuse RATING_NOT_FOUND "No rating exists for this aspect by the user."


**queries**

_getRatingsForGame (game: Game) : (ratings: set of Rating)

    returns all submitted ratings for the given game.


_getUserRatings (user: User) : (ratings: set of Rating)

    returns all aspect ratings submitted by the specified user.


---

**concept** AspectFiltering [Game, Aspect]

**purpose** compute aggregate aspect scores and filter games based on user-defined dimension thresholds

**principle** given a set of required aspects and minimum score thresholds, returns all games whose aggregate score for each specified aspect meets or exceeds the threshold

**state**

    a set of Criteria with
        an aspect Aspect
        a minScore Number

**actions**

setFilter (aspect: Aspect, minScore: Number) : ()

    where minScore >= 1 and minScore <= 10

    then
        if an entry in Criteria exists for aspect
            set minScore of entry to minScore
        otherwise
            add a new entry to Criteria with aspect and minScore


    where minScore < 1 or minScore > 10

    then
        refuse INVALID_THRESHOLD "Threshold score must be between 1 and 10."


clearFilters () : ()

    then
        remove all entries from Criteria


**queries**

_matchGames (ratings: set of AspectRating.Ratings) : (games: set of Game)

    returns all games where for every criterion in Criteria, the average score in ratings for that game's aspect is >= minScore.


---

### Essential Reactions

1. Synchronizing rating updates with discovery filtering:

**reaction** ReviewAspect

    when
        AspectRating.rateAspect (user, game, aspect, score, microNote)

    then
        AspectFiltering._matchGames (ratings: AspectRating.Ratings)


2. Matching catalog games when dimensional filters change:

**reaction** FilterCatalog

    when
        AspectFiltering.setFilter (aspect, minScore)

    then
        AspectFiltering._matchGames (ratings: AspectRating.Ratings)

3. Access control for submitting aspect ratings:

**reaction** AuthenticateBeforeRating

    when
        AspectRating.rateAspect (user, game, aspect, score, microNote)

    then
        UserAuthenticating._getUser (user.username)

## 5. User Journey

You work 40 hours a week and only have about 8 hours of gaming time available per week. You recently bought two $70 AAA titles with awesome cinematic visuals but a disappointing and shallow story, so you are determined not to waste your limited leisure time or money on another unenjoyable game. Standard review sites offer only aggregate star ratings, which hide whether a score is low due to poor combat mechanics or merely modest graphics.

Seeking a game with a deeply engaging narrative and rich world-building regardless of its budget or production polish, you open Gamexplorer and navigate to the Discovery page (Screen 2). Rather than typing vague terms like "story-driven" into a search bar, you check the filter box for Narrative Depth and set the minimum score slider to 8/ 10. You leave raw combat complexity and visual requirements unconstrained, allowing the platform to evaluate games based on storytelling quality.

Gamexplorer executes the query and highlights an indie RPG with an overall average of 5.5/10 stars on most mainstream aggregate sites because it has retro pixel art visuals and simple turn based combat. On Gamexplorer, however, you see that its average Narrative Depth score is and impressive 9.0/10 across 58 story-focused reviewers.

Intrigued, you click on the game cover to open its Game Detail page (Screen 1). Then, you expand the Narrative Depth tab to read targeted mini reviews submitted by previous players that describe how the story was gripping throughout every part of the game. This targeted feedback gives you confidence in purchasing the game without having to read through pages of lengthy and potentially spoiler-filled prose reviews.

You are convinced that the game fits your exact priorities, so you purchase and play the game. You thoroughly enjoy a rich 15-hour story. After completing the main narrative, you return to Gamexplorer, open the game's page, select Narrative Depth, and submit a score of 9 / 10 along with a short review praising the well-written character dialogue. Because Gamexplorer permits sparse ratings, you leave the Visual Polish and Combat Complexity dimensions unrated, avoiding the need to penalize the developer for budget limitations or distort the game's overall breakdown. With Gamexplorer you were able to discover and enjoy a wonderful game, in addition to sharing your love for the game with other like-minded gamers.
